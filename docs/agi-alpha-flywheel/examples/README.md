# A capability and earnings can jointly enable a successor

This exact-arithmetic illustration accompanies the AGI ALPHA FLYWHEEL paper. It enumerates a deliberately constructed set of counterfactuals. It is a **pedagogical calculation**, not an empirical business experiment, an autonomous discovery, a revenue forecast or a second enterprise ledger. It has no network access, financial-account integration or persistent operating state. The canonical enterprise continues to use its existing native accounts.

From the repository root:

```sh
python3 docs/agi-alpha-flywheel/examples/succession.py
python3 docs/agi-alpha-flywheel/examples/succession.py --check
```

The first command emits deterministic compact JSON. Its saved output is [example-results.json](example-results.json). The second independently checks arithmetic identities and the stated counterfactual outcomes, then compares the saved result with the current source. It reports an arithmetic verification, not a benchmark score.

## The constructed case

Every number is an exact integer in **abstract units**. Initial cash is 10. The predecessor pays complete costs of 8, including constructing and validating a reusable instrument, and collects 14 after accepted delivery. Its profit is 6 and cash is 16. All expenses are immediately paid; there is no debt or financing facility. Taxes, support, maintenance, refunds and financing costs are zero by definition in this abstract construction. They are not estimated or measured business costs; real economic evaluation must include them when applicable. There are no residual obligations unless a case explicitly introduces a cash restriction.

The successor requires 18 without the instrument or 15 with it. These are the two declared methods, not proven global cost minima. Both face the same outcome requirement.

| Instrument available | Earned cash available | Available cash | Required cash | Funded admission |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| No | No | 10 | 18 | Defer |
| No | Yes | 16 | 18 | Defer |
| Yes | No | 10 | 15 | Defer |
| Yes | Yes | 16 | 15 | Proceed |

The cash-withholding intervention earmarks the earned 6. It does not remove cash or erase the predecessor's profit. These successor counterfactuals share one historical predecessor calculation; they are separate explanatory arms, not money copied between operating accounts.

The inequalities `10 < 15 <= 16 < 18` make both inheritance and earned liquidity necessary **within these declared methods**. Funded admission does not establish acceptance. If the successor is accepted and collects 20, it earns 5; total cash becomes 21 and total profit 11. The instrument's original cost is counted once in the predecessor's 8.

## What the edge cases establish

- **Held earnings:** profit remains 6, but available cash is 10, so the investment of 15 waits.
- **Delayed receivable:** assume the predecessor's revenue of 14 is earned and recognized under this illustration's accounting rule, but collection is delayed. Profit remains 6, cash is 2 and the receivable is 14. The receivable cannot finance current spending. This illustrative recognition rule does not redefine native-account revenue policy.
- **Protection reserve:** earmarking 2 of the collected cash leaves 14 available. The profitable investment still waits. The reserve is a cash restriction, not an extra expense.
- **Failed successor acceptance:** paying 15 followed by refusal produces no successor revenue. Its cost remains sunk: combined cash is 1 and combined profit is -9.
- **An ordinary route without the inherited instrument:** admitting a conventional method costing 15 makes the successor fundable with 16 cash even when the inherited instrument is unavailable. This expands the declared alternative set and falsifies instrument necessity; the original threshold hypothesis no longer holds.
- **Strong conventional reuse:** an ordinary method given the same instrument, resources and costs ties exactly. The example therefore establishes no distinctive discovery-method advantage.

The four-cell interaction is a conditional threshold effect. It is not a universal claim that capabilities and capital are complementary. A full invention comparison must separately allow strong complete alternatives to construct their own means from common initial resources, permissions, financing choices and time budgets.
