# Can fewer electrical measurements identify a bubble column’s inlet state?

**Proposed retrospective experiment.** This design has not been registered or executed. The decision is whether an operator without access to inlet telemetry can identify the active gas-injection ports and the nearest commanded flow setting from a short electrical recording. When the valve settings and mass-flow controller are available, reading them directly resolves the recorded inlet state.

## Available evidence

The Hamburg experiment used a 104 mm diameter bubble column, eight rings of 32 electrodes and four excitation frequencies. Separately controlled spargers established the port configuration. A Bronkhorst mass-flow controller supplied the total flow reference. The coarse series covered 17 configurations and 12 flow settings from 1.0 to 6.5 L/min in 0.5 L/min steps. The researchers already tested reconstruction-free prediction. Their full-array method lost accuracy on unseen injection patterns, with a reported mean absolute flow-velocity error of 2.06 mm/s under predeployment calibration. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.iecr.6c01116) · [Data and acquisition details](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21773393)

A bounded read of the public archive verified four training-array headers. The measurement array contains 14,284 frames × 65,536 float32 values, paired with 14,284 port labels and 14,284 total-flow labels. A 204 × 2 integer array identifies contiguous acquisition segments. Inspection read 137,950 bytes and examined no held-out outcomes. The recordings, labels and published benchmark are already public. A new comparison could test a frozen rule on groups withheld from that comparison. Those groups would still come from the original apparatus and campaign. [Archive contents](https://zenodo.org/records/21773393/preview/measurement_data_bubbly_flow.zip?include_deleted=0)

## Proposed comparison

1. Before inspecting scores, freeze the grouping, split, preprocessing, estimators and refusal rule. Hold out entire port-configuration/flow-setting combinations from calibration and tuning. Keep every frame from the same combination together across the archive’s existing partitions. Evaluate each held-out combination once so long recordings do not dominate.
2. Compare the single ring nearest the inlets with the published full-array raw-signal method on the same held-out groups, frequencies and calibration budget. Permit both implementations the same operator assistance. Separately score the valve/controller readout wherever it is available. KTC phantom segmentation provides neither a port-state nor a flow measurement for this task.
3. Score one decision per group. It succeeds only if the exact active-port set matches and absolute flow error is below 0.25 L/min, half one coarse setting interval. This is an experimental resolution criterion. Count every refusal as an unsuccessful decision. Report paired wins, losses, ties, refusals, absolute flow error, measured input volume and processing time.

**Stopping rule.** Stop the reduced-ring comparison if it loses any group the full-array method resolves, increases refusals, or fails to reduce input volume. Retain the controller readout as the incumbent wherever it answers the operating question. Even a technical pass supports only retrospective inlet-state inference. Decline investment in a fault-detection service on this evidence. A trial of an inspection alarm would require independently measured per-port delivery during actual faults, a site-defined action threshold, and costs for missed faults and unnecessary inspections. These data do not establish that fewer recorded channels reduce installed costs, that an alarm improves operation, or that a buyer would pay.
